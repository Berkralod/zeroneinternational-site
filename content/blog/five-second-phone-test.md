Pick up your phone, open your own website and count to five. In that time, could a stranger tell what you do, where you do it and how to reach you?

That small test says a lot about how many customers your website wins or loses. Most people who look you up will do it on a phone, often while they are already on their way somewhere. This post explains why the phone version of your site is the one that counts, how to run the five-second test properly, and the fixes that usually cost little or nothing.

## Why the phone version is the one that counts

Two facts from Google explain it.

- **People who search nearby act fast.** Google reports that [76% of people who search on their smartphone for something nearby visit a related business within a day](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/data/mobile-near-me-searches/). Your website is often one of the last things they check before they decide.
- **Google looks at your mobile site first.** Google's own guide to [mobile-first indexing](https://developers.google.com/search/mobile-sites/) says it uses the mobile version of a site's content for indexing and ranking. If something is missing or broken on the phone version, it may as well be missing for Google too.

So the desktop version you see at the office is not the version most customers, or Google, judge you by.

## How to run the five-second test

Do it the way a new customer would, not the way you see your site every day:

1. **Use your phone, not a computer.** Close the site in your browser first so you start fresh.
2. **Turn off Wi-Fi.** Use mobile data, like someone standing in a parking lot.
3. **Start from Google.** Search for your business name or your main service and your town, then tap through to your site.
4. **Count to five, then look away.** Write down what you remember.
5. **Ask someone else.** A friend or family member who doesn't know your business will notice things you no longer see.

After five seconds, the visitor should be able to answer four questions:

- **What do you do?** In plain words, not a slogan.
- **Where?** Your town, county or service area.
- **How do I reach you?** A button they can tap to call, text or book.
- **Are you open?** Your hours, or at least when you answer.

If any answer needs scrolling, zooming or guessing, that is the first thing to fix.

## What should be visible without scrolling

The top of the screen on a phone is small, so every part of it has to earn its place. A good first screen usually has:

- Your business name and one line that says what you do and where ("Kitchen and bath remodeling in Suffolk County").
- One main button. For most local businesses that is **Call**. For others it is **Book** or **Get a quote**. One clear button beats four small ones.
- Your hours or "Open today until 6 pm", if customers usually call before they come.

What to move further down, or remove: large sliders that rotate through photos, autoplay videos, newsletter pop-ups that cover the page in the first seconds, and long welcome paragraphs.

## Speed: what the numbers say

A clear page that loads slowly still loses people. Google's research found that [53% of mobile site visits are abandoned if a page takes longer than three seconds to load](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/consumer-insights/consumer-trends/mobile-site-load-time-statistics/). That study is from 2017, and phones and networks are faster today. But so are customers' expectations, and the principle has not changed: every extra second gives people a reason to tap the back button and try the next business.

Google now measures page experience with three [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds). You don't need to understand the technical details. These are the targets Google calls "good":

- **Largest Contentful Paint (loading):** the main content appears within **2.5 seconds**.
- **Interaction to Next Paint (responsiveness):** the page reacts to a tap within **200 milliseconds**.
- **Cumulative Layout Shift (stability):** the page doesn't jump around while it loads, with a score of **0.1 or less**.

### Check your own site for free

Google's [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) is free. Paste your website address, press Analyze and look at the **Mobile** tab first. If your site has enough visitors, the top part shows what real Chrome users experienced. The lower part is a lab test with a list of suggestions. Don't chase a perfect score of 100. Aim for green or close to it on the three measures above.

## Fixes that usually cost little

Most slow or confusing small business sites have the same handful of problems:

- **Huge photos.** A photo straight from a phone camera can be several megabytes. Resized and compressed for the web, it often looks the same at a small fraction of the size.
- **Too many add-ons.** Chat widgets, sliders, animation libraries and old tracking codes all add weight. Remove what nobody uses.
- **A phone number that isn't a link.** On a phone, your number should start a call with one tap. The same goes for your address opening a map.
- **Contact details inside images.** Text in a picture can't be tapped, copied or read well by Google. Put your phone number, hours and service area in real text.
- **Tiny text and buttons.** If you have to zoom to read or tap, so do your customers.
- **Details that don't match your Google profile.** Your hours, phone number and service area should be the same on your website and your [Google Business Profile](/blog/google-business-profile-service-area-business/). Mismatches confuse customers.

## A 10-minute checklist

Print this or keep it on your phone:

- [ ] On a phone, the first screen says what we do and where.
- [ ] There is one clear button to call, text or book, and it works.
- [ ] Our hours are easy to find.
- [ ] Our phone number and address are tappable links.
- [ ] The site loads quickly on mobile data, not only on Wi-Fi.
- [ ] PageSpeed Insights (Mobile) shows green or close to green.
- [ ] No pop-up covers the page in the first seconds.
- [ ] Hours, phone number and service area match our Google Business Profile.
