Liquidation pallets can be one of the cheapest ways to source inventory, and one of the fastest ways to lose money. The difference is rarely luck. It is whether you did the math *before* you placed the bid. This guide walks through the method we use to decide whether a lot is worth buying and how much to pay for it.

## 1. Start with the manifest, not the photos

Photos show you a stack of boxes. The manifest tells you what is actually inside. Before anything else, download it and check three things:

- **Is it item-level?** A manifest that lists every item with a product name, identifier (UPC, ASIN or model number), quantity and retail price is far more useful than one that only lists categories.
- **How complete is it?** Count the lines and compare them with the stated unit count. A big gap means part of the lot is unknown, so treat it as worth nothing in your estimate.
- **Where does the "retail value" come from?** The total retail figure is usually the original list price. It says little about what the items sell for today, especially used or open-box.

## 2. Understand the condition grade

The same product can be worth very different amounts depending on where it came from:

- **Overstock and shelf pulls** are usually new or close to new.
- **Customer returns** are mixed: some items are perfect, some are missing parts, some do not work.
- **Salvage** or "as is" lots are mostly for parts and repair.

Your estimate has to reflect this. A simple way is to apply a condition factor to the resale price of each item, for example assuming that only a share of customer returns will sell as "like new" and the rest at a lower used price, or not at all.

## 3. Estimate what each item really sells for

For every line on the manifest that matters, look up **recent sold prices** on the marketplaces you sell on, not current asking prices. Sold listings show what buyers actually paid. Note two numbers per item:

1. A realistic sale price for the condition you expect.
2. How quickly it sells (sell-through). An item that sells in a week is worth more to you than one that sits for six months, because unsold stock ties up cash and space.

You do not need to research every single line by hand. Sort the manifest by retail value and research the items that make up most of the value first. Often a fifth of the lines carry most of the lot's worth.

## 4. Subtract every cost, not just the bid

The profit on a lot is what is left after all of these:

- The winning bid plus buyer's premium and taxes.
- Freight to your location.
- Marketplace and payment fees on each sale.
- Shipping supplies and outbound shipping you pay yourself.
- Your time: testing, cleaning, photographing, listing and packing.
- Items you cannot sell and have to donate, recycle or throw away.

A useful habit is to put a value on your own hour. If a lot needs forty hours of work to process, those hours belong in the calculation.

## 5. Work backwards to a maximum bid

Once you have an estimated total net resale value (after condition, fees and unsellable items), decide what margin you need for the risk. Then work backwards:

> **Maximum bid** = expected net resale value − freight − processing costs − the profit you require

**An illustrative example (all numbers assumed):** a lot is expected to bring in $4,000 after fees and unsellable items. Freight is $350, supplies and labor are valued at $900, and you want at least $1,200 profit for the risk. Your maximum bid, including the buyer's premium, is $4,000 − $350 − $900 − $1,200 = **$1,550**. If the auction goes above that, you let it go.

Writing the maximum down before the auction ends is the single best protection against bidding with your emotions.

## 6. Watch for red flags

- Manifests with many lines marked "unknown", "assorted" or "mixed".
- A few very expensive items carrying most of the stated value. If one of them is missing or broken, the lot collapses.
- Products that are restricted, recalled or hard to sell on your marketplaces.
- Categories you know nothing about. Your price research will be weaker, so demand a bigger margin.
